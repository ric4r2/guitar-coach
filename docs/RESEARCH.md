# GuitarCoach: research summary

Research date: 16 Nov 2026. This summarizes public course descriptions and the structure of the
user's book. It does not copy book content.

## 1. Berklee

### Guitar method (the core of the guitar department)
- **William Leavitt, *A Modern Method for Guitar*, Vols. 1–3.** Leavitt chaired Berklee's guitar
  department from 1965 to 1990, and the method is still the department's basic text. It is written in
  standard notation (tab only in newer editions), so reading is learned from day one.
  - *Vol. 1:* open and first positions, reading in C, G, F and their relative minors, major scales in
    position, basic rhythms, first chords and arpeggios, picking technique, duets.
  - *Vol. 2:* the whole fingerboard by position, all major/minor scales in several fingerings,
    intervals, triads and seventh-chord arpeggios, chord voicings, rhythm guitar, a first look at
    improvisation, play-along duets.
  - *Vol. 3:* advanced scales and arpeggios, chord/scale relationships, chord construction, extended
    voicings, rhythm guitar styles.
- **Leavitt, *Melodic Rhythms for Guitar*.** Rhythm reading in the styles of contemporary music:
  68 short harmonized melodies and 42 rhythm exercises, graded from whole notes to syncopated
  sixteenths and triplets.
- **Leavitt, *Reading Studies* / *Advanced Reading Studies*.** Sight-reading in every key, positions
  1–7, then 8–12.
- Department emphasis (from Berklee's "Studying Guitar" pages): tone, technique, scales, harmony,
  reading, improvisation, phrasing and groove, repertoire. Guitar Labs (reading, rhythm guitar,
  styles) run alongside private lessons.

### Core music curriculum (all majors)
- **Harmony 1–4**: notation and chord symbols → diatonic harmony in major, Roman numerals, guide
  tones, voice leading → secondary and extended dominants, II-V relationships, minor-key harmony,
  modal interchange, substitute dominants → deceptive resolutions, non-functional dominants, modal
  harmony, compound chords. Reharmonization is a later elective.
- **Ear Training 1–4**: solfège (moveable do), rhythm reading and dictation, melodic dictation,
  intervals, chord qualities, then harmonic ear training (bass lines and progressions).
- **Berklee Online** mirrors this in three levels: Music Theory 101 (notation, scales, intervals,
  triads, rhythm), 201 (harmony and function), 301 (advanced melody, harmony, rhythm); Ear Training
  Fundamentals → ET 1 → ET 2 → Harmonic Ear Training (recognizing progressions).
- **Berklee Online *Blues Guitar*** (Level 2, 12 weeks): 12-bar harmony and rhythm guitar, E minor
  pentatonic and blues scale in open position and then up the neck, shuffles, straight-eighth and slow
  blues in E, G and other keys, 8- and 12-bar forms. *Advanced Blues Guitar* adds funky and soul
  blues grooves and solo development.

## 2. Other top programs
- **Musicians Institute (GIT, Los Angeles).** Core classes: Guitar Technique (finger independence,
  coordination, speed), Reading for Guitar (notation, chord charts, rhythm), Harmony/Theory/Ear
  Training, Performance. *Fretboard mastery* is named as a primary focus, taught through the
  *Fretboard Biology* sequence (note names, CAGED shapes, intervals, then rhythm notation and chart
  reading).
- **Common ground** across Berklee, MI and conservatory jazz programs: (1) know every note on the neck,
  (2) read rhythm before pitch, (3) intervals as both sound and shape, (4) scales and arpeggios in five
  connected positions, (5) diatonic harmony and Roman numerals, (6) blues as the first improvising
  form, (7) daily technique with a metronome, raised in small tempo steps.

## 3. The user's book: Jim Gleason, *Guitar Improv & Comping* (gi.pdf)
Read: table of contents (pp. 1–20), the study-method chapter, the "Globalizing" introduction,
and samples of *Rhythmic Words* and *Blues Rock and Swing Blues Cells*. About 3,700 pages,
10 parts. The author states it is **not for beginners: about a year of basic study** first.

**Structure.** Melodic Improv (Parts 1–7) then Harmonic Improv (Parts 8–9), then summaries and quizzes.
1. Starting to Improvise: fretboard letter names, reading notation, rhythmic words, speed reading
   pitch, chord progression by number, basic chord construction, playing in position, pentatonic
   scales and octave shapes, blues-rock and swing-blues cells, improvising by ear.
2. Comping design and pentatonic improv: keys and key signatures, 12-bar blues endings, keeping
   time, pentatonic and major scale fingering, spontaneous rhythmic phrases.
3. Designing solos: triad arpeggios, scale-tone arpeggios, chord tones over the whole neck, metric
   phrasing, setup and target phrases.
4. Chords and modes: naming conventions, scale-tone chords, modes, parent scales, substitution,
   secondary dominants, modal II-V-I.
5–7. Core melody, melodic cells (fragments, thirds/sixths, double stops, chromatic neighbours,
   diminished cells), phrases, bebop ornamentation, chord melody.
8–9. Chord progression and comping, bass lines, voice leading, cadences.
10. Summaries of major, harmonic minor, melodic minor and harmonic major, plus quizzes.

**Key ideas the app must prepare for** (my summary of the author's vocabulary):
- **Numbers, not letters.** Melodies, chords and progressions are thought of as scale degrees
  (1, b3, 5, 6...) and Roman numerals, so they move to any key.
- **Rhythmic words.** Named rhythm cells: three-pulse (triplet, swing eighths, "Afro-Cuban" = first
  two parts of a triplet, "waltz" = last two) and four-pulse (all four, "gallop", "jingle bells",
  "Creedence" and others). Learned by air-strumming while speaking, then clapping and counting.
- **Five octave shapes** named after the open E, D, C, A and G chords (the CAGED idea), and two
  versions of most one-octave patterns ("forward slash" and "back slash").
- **Cells and licks** labelled by the scale degrees they target (for example 5-6-1-3), organised by
  octave shape, in blues-rock and swing-blues feels.
- **Globalizing:** learn an idea, name its category, then re-apply it to other keys, scales, chords,
  rhythms and fingerings.
- **Practice method:** short phrases (8–20 events), start at a tempo with 5–10% errors and speed up
  only when clean, repeat until fatigue then rest half as long, 5–10 cycles, and space repetitions at
  growing intervals. The app's practice timer and review scheduling follow this.

**Readiness checklist for the book (target: 12–18 months):** read single-line notation in position,
name any fret instantly, hear and play intervals, play major and minor pentatonic, blues and major
scales in five shapes, build triads and seventh chords, know diatonic chords and numbers in every key,
count and play rhythmic words, play a 12-bar blues in several feels and solo over it with licks.
